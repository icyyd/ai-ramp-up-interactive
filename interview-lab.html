Do not say

  • Never imply Morgan Stanley-scale telemetry operations. Name the boundary, then pivot to what you do bring.
  • The AI agent work is a proof of concept. Keep proposed architecture, implemented controls, and measured results separate.
  • Do not claim controls outside your scope. Say the CrowdStrike coverage model you worked with, not ownership of the whole control.
  • No invented precision. A concrete control flow beats a number you cannot source.
  • Keep attribution exact. "My team rolled out" and "I designed" are different claims.
  • Do not open with the tool name. Control objective first, product as evidence second.
  • Proposals are not history. Everything in the point-of-view section is “here is how I would approach it,” never “here is what I built.”

Ask them

  • How is the platform measured today — findings created, or risk actually retired?
  • Where does the platform's responsibility end and the application team's begin?
  • What is the largest source of noise in the finding pipeline right now?
  • How are exceptions and their expiry governed at your scale?
  • How does the team split between building the platform and running the operating model?
  • What would you want visibly different ninety days in?
  • Where does agentic tooling sit on the roadmap, and who owns its guardrails?
  • What can a developer do with AI today without asking permission — and where does that line sit?

Last thirty seconds

What I have built is the operating model underneath this mission: controls that live in the engineering workflow, findings that reach an accountable owner, and evidence that exists because the system produced it. Morgan Stanley adds the scale and the risk discipline — that is the part I want to work at next.

Core talk tracks
60 seconds
Elevator pitch

I am a cloud platform and security engineering leader who has owned roadmap, architecture, and delivery across AWS, Azure, and GCP. At Tricentis, I shipped an internal developer platform that cut provisioning from weeks to hours and grew the team from 3 to 14 supporting more than 150 developers.

My security work spans the delivery lifecycle: GitGuardian across all company organizations and repositories, Snyk controls before merge and deployment, Rapid7 vulnerability remediation, CrowdStrike Falcon coverage for Kubernetes, and SOC 2 audit participation. Most recently, I refactored privileged access through Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management and access packages to remove standing production access, simplify IT provisioning, and automate access review evidence. This role fits because it needs someone who can turn that security data and policy into a platform application teams will actually use.

45 seconds
Describe your security depth

I think about cloud security in four connected layers. First is identity: least privilege, JIT elevation, governed provisioning, and reviewable access. Second is software delivery: GitGuardian and Snyk preventing secrets and vulnerable code from entering protected branches. Third is runtime: Kubernetes workload coverage with CrowdStrike Falcon and cloud-native controls. Fourth is governance: Rapid7 remediation workflows, SOC 2 evidence, exceptions, and accountable owners. My role has been to connect those layers into the platform and operating model, not run them as separate security projects.

30 seconds
Why Morgan Stanley and this role

The role combines the parts of my background that are usually separated: multi-cloud platforms, security engineering, data products, developer experience, and people leadership. The mission that stands out is delivering curated, actionable security guidance to application teams. I have implemented the underlying controls and operating model; Morgan Stanley adds the scale, telemetry depth, and risk discipline to build that into a mature security data platform.

30 seconds
How hands-on are you?

I stay hands-on at the architecture, technical spike, design-review, and code-review level. The recent identity redesign is a good example: I worked through the JIT access model, Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management enablement, access-package lifecycle, and audit path with the team and IT. I build or validate the first path when that retires uncertainty, then create clear engineering ownership so I do not become the delivery bottleneck.

Credibility
Bridge the data-engineering boundary

My depth is large-scale cloud platforms, security posture, and developer-facing products. I understand durable ingestion, schema evolution, idempotency, replay, quality, lineage, and tiered serving, and I have led governed data and machine-learning products. I have not operated a security telemetry platform at Morgan Stanley's volume, so I would not overstate that. What I bring is the ability to define the decision, architecture, standards, and team operating model with the data engineers who will run it.

Leadership
Handle technical disagreement

I make the constraints explicit and compare designs against security boundary, failure modes, operational cost, reversibility, and delivery time. If an engineer's design wins against those constraints, we use it even when it is not my stylistic preference. Reversible decisions get a bounded experiment; changes to identity or another hard-to-reverse security boundary require stronger evidence and a documented decision.

Governance · 60 seconds
AI freedom for developers without losing control

I would start by separating two things that usually get conflated: which model a developer uses, and what the system is allowed to touch. Restricting the first buys almost no risk reduction and costs you adoption — and the usage does not stop, it moves to personal accounts where you have no visibility at all.

So I would make exploration genuinely free: any approved model, sandboxed, non-production data, default-on, no ticket. Then concentrate governance where actions become consequential — an agent holding production credentials, or one that can write. Those tiers get a scoped identity, tools from a reviewed catalog, a deterministic policy check on every call, human approval for material writes, and an expiry on the authorization.

The way you make it stick is the same lesson as the identity work: the governed path has to be the fastest path. If the paved road hands you guardrails, logging, and identity for free, teams take it because it is quicker, not because a policy told them to.

JD tie: actionable developer guidance, developer-facing platform, governance, and technical strategy in one answer.
Agentic · 45 seconds
How would you secure an agentic system

Four questions, ordered by how much damage the answer can do. What identity is it acting as — a scoped workload identity with short-lived credentials, never a shared key, read separated from write. What can it call — tools from a reviewed catalog, with a deterministic policy check before execution rather than the model deciding whether it is allowed. What does it trust — retrieved documents and tool output are data, never instructions, which is the prompt-injection boundary. And what happens afterward — one audit record per action covering model and prompt version, context, tool calls, approvals, and outcome, plus a revocation path that works in seconds.

The unifying principle is that the model may propose anything, but the control plane stays deterministic.

AppSec · 30 seconds
Is reviewing AI-generated code different

The pipeline should not care who wrote it — the same secret scanning, dependency analysis, tests, and review apply. What changes is volume and review posture. You get more code, faster, and it looks plausible, which is exactly the condition under which human review quietly degrades into approval. So I would watch two signals: whether review depth holds as volume rises, and whether the author can explain the change they are submitting. The other real shift is supply chain — generated code will confidently invent a plausible package name, so dependency verification carries more weight than it used to.

Accountability · 30 seconds
Who is responsible when an agent acts badly

The person or team who authorized it — and that has to be a named owner, not “the platform.” Which means the authorization must be an explicit, revocable, expiring grant rather than something that accumulated quietly over time. Every agent gets a registered owner, a defined blast radius, and a review date. When something goes wrong the questions are the ordinary incident questions: what identity, which permission allowed it, what evidence exists, what is the revert path. My insistence on reversibility at the highest autonomy tier is not philosophical — accountability without a revert path is just blame.

Technical cheatsheet · live explanation

Name the five stages, one example each, then let them choose where to go deeper.

Design a security telemetry and developer-guidance platform

Start with the decision: an application team needs to know what to fix first, why it matters, and how to fix it. I would collect signals from cloud platforms, Rapid7, CrowdStrike, GitGuardian, Snyk, CI/CD, Microsoft Entra, and the asset catalog. I would normalize them into a common finding model, then add owner, service criticality, exposure, reachability, identity context, and compensating controls. A versioned policy layer would produce an explainable priority and due date. Finally, I would deliver that guidance in the developer workflow and measure whether teams acted. Detection and compliance can reuse the same governed data, but with their own latency and retention requirements.
If they go deeper
Reliability, governance, and access controls
  • Reliability: durable buffering, at-least-once delivery, stable finding IDs, idempotent consumers, dead-letter handling, and replay from immutable raw storage.
  • Data quality: source coverage, freshness, completeness, schema version, deduplication, enrichment misses, and owner-mapping confidence.
  • Governance: data classification, retention by purpose, lineage, source evidence, transformation version, and auditable policy decisions.
  • Access: workload identity, least privilege, JIT elevation for operators, separation of duties, and controlled access to sensitive telemetry.
  • Success: time to triage, time to remediate, overdue material exposure, exception age, action rate, and risk retired—not the number of findings created.

Explain vulnerability priority

  • Urgent: known exploited, exposed or reachable, and on a material service.
  • Short SLA: high exploit likelihood or attack-path relevance on an important service.
  • Planned: serious severity but not currently exposed or reachable.
  • Monitor or accept: low evidence, with a named owner, rationale, compensating control, and expiry.

Signals and measurement

  • CVSS for technical severity
  • CISA KEV for known exploitation
  • EPSS for near-term exploit likelihood
  • Rapid7 finding state and remediation evidence
  • Exposure, reachability, asset criticality, identity path, and controls
  • Measure risk retired, overdue exposure, reopen rate, and exception aging
Cloud security program model

The spine answer for “how would you run this.” Five planes, one governing loop, and an explicit column for what AI changes — so enablement is part of the model rather than an exception to it.

Secure defaults, provable outcomes

Every plane answers the same three questions: what is the control objective, how is it enforced automatically, and what evidence does it leave behind. A control that depends on someone remembering is not a control; a control that produces no evidence cannot be audited; and a control that makes the secure path slower will be routed around.

PlaneControl objectiveWhat AI changes
IdentityEvery actor, human or machine, is named, least-privileged, and time-boundAgents are non-human identities. Scoped workload identity, short-lived credentials, read separated from write, no shared keys, just-in-time for anything privileged.
DeliveryPrevent at the point of change rather than at a release gateGenerated code passes the same gates as written code. Volume rises, so review depth becomes something you measure; dependency verification matters more because models invent plausible packages.
RuntimeObserve and contain what is actually deployedAn agent's tool calls are runtime events. Bounded blast radius, reversible actions, every call logged, and a revocation path tested like break-glass.
DataClassify, minimize, and control where data comes to restPrompt and retrieved context are an egress path. Retrieval inherits source permissions and never widens them; sensitive data is filtered before it reaches a model, not after.
AssuranceProve continuously that the control operatedEvidence extends to model and prompt version, retrieved context, tool calls, and approvals. The system must also be able to decline: insufficient evidence is a valid result.
Secure default beats secure policyPolicy documents get read once. Defaults get used every day. If the paved path is not the fastest path, you have written a policy, not built a control.
Govern the action, not the explorationFriction belongs where writes and production data are, not where a developer is trying something. Restricting exploration only relocates it out of view.
Keep the control plane deterministicThe model may propose anything. Enforcement stays deterministic, reviewable, and independent of the model's judgement.
If they ask where you would start: identity first, because it bounds every other plane and it is the hardest to retrofit. Then delivery gates, then runtime containment, then data controls. Assurance is not a phase — each plane ships with its evidence, or it is not done.

Live line: “AI does not replace the control plane. It makes least privilege, deterministic gates, human approval, traceability, and rollback more important, because one incorrect decision can now execute at machine speed.”
Point of view · what I would build here
These are proposals, not history. Introduce every one of them as forward-looking — “here is how I would approach it,” “the model I would propose” — never as work already delivered. At VP level the interviewer expects a point of view; they will lose trust instantly if a proposal is dressed up as a result.

Govern the blast radius, not the keyboard

The failure mode of restrictive AI policy is not that developers stop using AI. It is that the usage moves somewhere you cannot see, on personal accounts, with production data pasted into it. Governance should be almost invisible at the point of exploration and very strict at the point of consequence. That is the same lesson as the identity work: the control gets adopted when the governed path is also the fastest path.

Proposal one · A tiered autonomy model

One framework replaces the endless case-by-case debate. What matters is not which model a team uses, it is what the system is allowed to touch and whether the action can be undone.

TierWhat the developer getsWhat the platform requires in exchange
0 · ExploreAny approved model, sandboxed, synthetic or public dataNothing. Default-on for every engineer, no ticket, no review. This tier is where creative freedom lives and it should be genuinely unrestricted.
1 · AssistAI writing code in the IDE and in pull requestsNo new gate. The existing pipeline is the control: secret scanning, dependency and code analysis, tests, and human review apply exactly as they do to hand-written code.
2 · SupervisedAn agent reading production telemetry and proposing changesScoped read-only workload identity, registration in the tool catalog, and a human who merges. The agent proposes; it never applies.
3 · AutonomousAn agent acting without a human in each loopReversible actions only, an explicitly bounded blast radius, named accountable owner, approval for material writes, monitoring, and an expiry date on the authorization.
The line to say: “Tier 0 and tier 1 cover the overwhelming majority of developer usage, and I would deliberately put almost no friction there. The governance effort belongs at tiers 2 and 3, where an action becomes hard to reverse.”

Proposal two · The paved road that makes it real

Freedom comes from good defaults, not from permission. A team on the platform default should get identity, guardrails, logging, and an approved tool catalog for free — and reach production faster than a team rolling its own.

  • One governed model endpoint with Bedrock Guardrails attached centrally. Because ApplyGuardrail evaluates independently of the model call, teams keep free model choice while the control stays constant.
  • A distinct workload identity per agent with short-lived STS credentials, never a shared key — AgentCore Identity federates Microsoft Entra, so this extends the identity work rather than replacing it.
  • Tools from a reviewed catalog with deterministic Cedar policy on every call before it executes — AgentCore Registry and AgentCore Policy.
  • Material writes routed through existing CI/CD; agents open pull requests, never touch production directly. Tracing pre-wired via AgentCore Observability and Bedrock invocation logging.

Proposal three · The security data platform, in AWS services

Same five stages as the technical answer, made concrete. Use this only if they ask for AWS specifics, and lead with the stage, not the service name.

1 · Collect
CloudTrail management and data events, EKS audit logs, VPC Flow Logs, GuardDuty, Inspector for workload CVEs, Config, Security Hub — with Rapid7, CrowdStrike, GitGuardian, Snyk, and Entra arriving as custom sources.
2 · Normalize
Security Lake converts to the OCSF schema in Parquet on S3, catalogued through Glue and Lake Formation — the managed version of the common finding model.
3 · Context
Join ownership, service criticality, environment, and exposure from the asset catalog; Lake Formation governs who can read which columns of sensitive telemetry.
4 · Prioritize
A versioned policy layer over KEV, EPSS, reachability, and compensating controls, run in Athena or Lambda, emitting a priority with reason codes rather than a bare score.
5 · Deliver
EventBridge into Step Functions to open the pull request or ticket against the owning team, with human approval as an explicit state; adoption and risk retired measured in QuickSight or OpenSearch.
The senior signal: “Stages one and two are largely a managed capability now — Security Lake gives you OCSF normalization without building it. The differentiated engineering is stage three and four, ownership context and an explainable policy layer, and that is where I would put the team. I would not spend headcount rebuilding a normalizer.”
Eight-case STAR bank
Shift-left security across every repository
AppSec · developer guidance · secure SDLC
Situation
Secrets and vulnerable code were most expensive when found after merge or deployment, and coverage varied across company organizations and repositories.
Task
Create consistent prevention without turning Security into a manual release gate.
Action
My team rolled GitGuardian across all company organizations and repositories and implemented Snyk in the same shift-left model. Checks ran in the engineering workflow so high-confidence secret, code, dependency, and Infrastructure as Code findings could be addressed before protected branches and deployment. We paired policy with central visibility, triage, developer guidance, and exception handling.
Result
Security feedback moved to the point of change and controls became consistently applied across the code estate rather than dependent on opt-in review.
Lesson
Separate new-code prevention from legacy backlog remediation and tune blocking thresholds before enforcement.
Proof to prepare: rollout scope and sequence, one real GitGuardian and Snyk finding, block-versus-warn policy, false-positive handling, and exception expiry.
Remove standing production access with JIT IAM
Identity · governance · cross-functional delivery
Situation
Standing production access increased risk, while manual provisioning created work for IT and inconsistent evidence for review.
Task
Reduce persistent privilege while making legitimate access faster and easier to govern.
Action
I worked on refactoring IAM through Microsoft Entra Privileged Identity Management so privileged roles became eligible and activated just in time. We used access packages to standardize request, approval, assignment, and expiry, streamlined IT provisioning, and introduced automated access auditing and review evidence through Entra.
Result
The design revoked standing production access, created a repeatable provisioning path for IT, and made privileged-access evidence easier to review.
Lesson
Identity controls are adopted when the secure path also improves the user and operator experience.
Proof to prepare: roles in scope, activation duration, approval and MFA requirements, access-package lifecycle, review cadence, break-glass handling, and before/after standing-access count if available.
Close the loop from runtime finding to audit evidence
Rapid7 · CrowdStrike · SOC 2 · remediation
Situation
The team supported Kubernetes runtime protection through CrowdStrike Falcon, received vulnerability findings through Rapid7, and had to demonstrate control operation during SOC 2 audits.
Task
Turn security-tool output into owned remediation and defensible evidence rather than disconnected alerts.
Action
We treated each finding as a lifecycle: validate and deduplicate it, map it to the deployed asset and owner, prioritize it by exposure and impact, assign remediation, record exceptions, verify closure, and preserve evidence. CrowdStrike coverage and Rapid7 remediation records supported the broader assurance story during SOC 2 audit participation.
Result
The operating model connected detection, ownership, remediation, and audit evidence, improving the team's ability to explain both risk and control effectiveness.
Lesson
A scanner is a source, not a vulnerability-management program; ownership data and closure evidence are the real control.
Proof to prepare: one Rapid7 finding, prioritization basis, remediation owner, validation method, CrowdStrike coverage boundary, and one SOC 2 evidence request you supported.

Four acquisitions: governance before re-platforming
Security strategy · regulated change · influence
Situation
Four acquisitions arrived with different cloud estates, identities, and security postures. Full re-platforming would take quarters while governance risk was immediate.
Task
Reduce risk without stalling integration or product roadmaps.
Action
Sequenced identity boundaries, logging and evidence, minimum guardrails, and accountable owners before workload migration. Presented the interim state as a time-bound risk burn-down plan.
